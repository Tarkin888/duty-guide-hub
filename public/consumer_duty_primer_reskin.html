Consumer Duty Primer
Embedded - orientation for the whole framework

Deliver good outcomes, and prove it.

The Consumer Duty is the FCA's standard of care for retail customers. It asks every firm to act to deliver good outcomes - and to hold evidence, not just policy, that shows it is happening. This primer walks you through what the Duty requires, how it is structured, and where firms most often fall short.

01 Why it matters

A shift from checklists to outcomes

The Duty moves regulation beyond "did you follow the process?" towards "can you show the customer was actually better off?" That change in question is the whole point.

✦

Raises the bar

Sets a higher, clearer standard of care than the old Treating Customers Fairly regime - firms must act to deliver good outcomes, not merely avoid poor ones.

✦

Outcomes over process

Firms are judged on the results customers actually experience - value received, understanding gained, support given - not on whether a policy document exists.

✦

Whole relationship, whole lifecycle

The Duty applies from product design through sale, communication and ongoing support - including to closed books, switching and cancelling.

02 Timeline

How the Duty has landed, step by step

Click any milestone for the detail. The Duty did not stop at go-live - the FCA continues to publish good and poor practice findings and adjust governance expectations.

31 October 2022
Implementation plans due
Firms were expected to have board-approved implementation plans in place, setting out how they would embed the Duty ahead of the first live date.
31 July 2023 Went live
Duty in force for open products and services
The Consumer Duty came into force for all new and existing products or services that were open to sale or renewal. From this date, the four outcomes and cross-cutting rules applied in full.
31 July 2024 Went live
Duty extended to closed products and services
Coverage extended to closed products and services - those no longer marketed or distributed, including by renewal, on or after 31 July 2023. A product only closed after that date is not treated as "closed" for Duty purposes.
27 February 2025 Governance
Board champion expectation removed
The FCA confirmed it no longer expects firms to have a dedicated Consumer Duty Board champion. Boards may retain the role if they judge it useful, but it is no longer a stated expectation in FCA guidance.
2025-26
Focus areas and simplification work
The FCA published its Consumer Duty focus areas for 2025/26, setting cross-cutting and sector priorities, alongside wider work to simplify Handbook requirements where it can without weakening protection.
July 2026
Latest good and poor practice findings
Recent publications cover outcomes monitoring, and products and services good practice - part of a continuing programme of thematic reviews rather than a one-off implementation exercise.
03 Scope

Who the Duty applies to

The Duty applies across the distribution chain, not just to the firm with the customer relationship.

  • 01Firms that manufacture, design or approve products and services for retail customers.
  • 02Firms that distribute, advise on or arrange those products and services, even without direct customer contact.
  • 03Firms serving retail customers directly, including consumers, and - in defined circumstances - small businesses and charities treated as retail clients.
  • 04Both open products sold today and closed products no longer on sale, since 31 July 2024.
Proportionality for smaller firms Smaller firms must still deliver the same good outcomes, but how they evidence this can reflect their size and complexity. The Duty is outcomes-based, not process-heavy - evidence should be proportionate, not identical to a large firm's.

05 Cross-cutting rules

How firms must behave, throughout

Three rules that sit beneath the Consumer Principle and inform how every one of the four outcomes should be interpreted.

1

Act in good faith towards customers

Treat customers honestly and fairly - this is the baseline standard of conduct expected in every interaction.

2

Avoid causing foreseeable harm

Identify and mitigate risks to customers before they materialise, rather than reacting once harm has occurred.

3

Enable and support financial objectives

Give customers clear information and effective support so they can pursue what they are actually trying to achieve.

06 The four outcomes

What firms must actually deliver

Each outcome covers a distinct part of the firm-customer relationship. Click to expand what the FCA wants, and what it expects to see in practice.

01

Products & services

+

What the FCA wants

Consumers sold products and services that meet their needs, characteristics and objectives, and that work the way they expect.

What is expected

A clearly defined target market, products suitable for that market, and regular monitoring to confirm they still meet customer needs.

02

Price & value

+

What the FCA wants

Products and services that offer fair value - a reasonable relationship between the price charged and the overall benefits received.

What is expected

Fair value is more than price: firms should regularly review fees and charges against benefits, limitations and non-financial costs, and avoid hidden costs or poor value propositions.

03

Consumer understanding

+

What the FCA wants

Consumers who understand the information they are given and can make timely, informed decisions.

What is expected

Clear, timely, accessible communications free of jargon, tailored to the customer's level of financial literacy, and tested to confirm key information actually lands.

04

Consumer support

+

What the FCA wants

Customers should not face unreasonable barriers to accessing support or switching providers.

What is expected

Support is as easy to access as sales. Clear, responsive, and accessible channels for help, switching and cancelling, without artificial friction or delay.

07 Governance

Ownership at the top

Consumer Duty is the board's responsibility. It is not a compliance-department project or a once-a-year report.

Board and Chair

The board must collectively own the Consumer Duty and actively scrutinise delivery. This is not delegable. The chair sets the tone, and boards must regularly challenge management on outcomes evidence.

Senior Management Functions

CEOs, Chief Risk Officers, and business-line leads each have Consumer Duty accountability within their domains. They must own the framework and evidence, not pass it down.

Annual board report and review

The FCA expects an annual board report on how the firm is delivering good outcomes. This is not a box to tick: boards must analyse the evidence, identify what is working and what is not, and drive action.

Continuous scrutiny

Boards are expected to review evidence on how the firm is delivering good outcomes at least annually, using it to identify issues, agree action and track remediation.

i

What changed on 27 February 2025: the FCA removed its expectation for a dedicated Consumer Duty Board champion, giving firms flexibility over governance arrangements. This did not reduce the underlying requirement for boards to discuss Duty performance and management information, and to properly scrutinise and challenge the annual Consumer Duty board report.

08 Monitoring & evidence

From dashboards to conclusions

The FCA's evidential bar has risen since go-live. In its April 2026 review of Year 2 board reports, it told firms to move past management-information dashboards to genuine analysis.

Collect

Management information

Complaints, cancellations, call data, vulnerability flags, product performance.

Analyse

Draw conclusions

Identify good and poor outcomes and understand their causes, not just report the numbers.

Challenge

Board scrutiny

Document the challenge boards actually give management, not just that a report was presented.

Act

Close the loop

Take appropriate action and track it through to a demonstrable improvement in outcomes.

09 Vulnerability & fair treatment

Understanding customers in vulnerable circumstances

Vulnerability is not a separate outcome - it runs through all four. The FCA expects firms to understand the needs of their customer base, including those with characteristics of vulnerability, and to respond accordingly.

H

Health

Conditions or illnesses affecting day-to-day capability, including mental health.

L

Life events

Bereavement, job loss, relationship breakdown or other significant life changes.

R

Resilience

Low financial resilience - limited ability to withstand financial shocks.

C

Capability

Low knowledge of financial matters, or low confidence managing money or using digital services.

10 Practice & pitfalls

What firms must do vs. what good looks like

Meeting the minimum and demonstrating genuine good practice are different bars. The comparison below illustrates the gap.

Target marketDefine a target market for each productGranular segmentation tested against real customer data, reviewed on a cycle
Fair valueComplete a fair value assessmentAssessments that compare price to benefits, limitations and non-financial costs, refreshed when terms change
CommunicationsAvoid misleading informationPlain-English testing with real customers, including those with low financial literacy
SupportProvide a way to contact the firmSupport as easy to access as sales, with no extra friction to switch or cancel

Common failure points

Click a flag for the detail the FCA has raised in its good and poor practice reviews.

⚑ Dashboard without analysis
MI is collected and presented, but no one draws a conclusion about what it means for customers or what should change.
⚑ Stale fair value assessments
Assessments completed once at launch and not revisited when pricing, benefits or the market moves.
⚑ Vulnerability not embedded
Vulnerability treated as a specialist team's job rather than built into product design, communications and support end to end.
⚑ Sludge in cancellation journeys
Signing up is fast; leaving, switching or getting help is deliberately or accidentally slower and harder.
⚑ Jargon-heavy communications
Key information is technically present but not actually understandable to the intended audience.
⚑ Governance without challenge
Board reports presented and noted, without evidence of genuine scrutiny, questioning or follow-up action.
